💡 Query, Key & Value Explained
Q Query
"What am I looking for?"

The question a token asks when seeking context from other words. It specifies what type of relationship or meaning it wants to connect with.

Example: In "The wizard placed the book on the altar because it was sacred", the word "it" queries: "Which previous noun is the thing that is sacred?"
K Key
"What information do I have?"

The tag or label every token broadcasts like a name tag, advertising its identity, grammatical role, and features to other tokens.

Example: The token "altar" advertises: "I am a singular holy object", whereas "book" advertises: "I am a portable reading object".
V Value
"What content do I share?"

The actual informational payload. When a Query strongly matches a Key, that token's Value vector is retrieved and blended into the Query token.

Example: Because "it" matches the Key for "altar", the rich meaning of "altar" gets mixed into the representation of "it".
📐 The Scaled Dot-Product Attention Formula
Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax( (Q · KT) / √dk ) · V

Why divide by √dk? When the vector dimension (dk) is large (e.g., 64, 128, or more), computing the dot product between Q and K results in very large numbers. Feeding huge numbers into the softmax function forces its outputs to become extremely peaked (one value near 1.0 and all others near 0), which causes the mathematical gradients to become dangerously tiny (the "vanishing gradient" problem). Dividing by √dk scales the variance back down to 1.0, preserving healthy gradients so the model can train and learn smoothly.

🔍 Multi-Head Attention

Allows each token to look at and gather information from all other tokens in the sequence simultaneously across multiple representation subspaces.

Why it's essential: Without attention, tokens are processed in total isolation. Attention is what injects dynamic context, syntax, and relational meaning.
MultiHead(Q,K,V) = Concat(head_1, ..., head_h) · W_O
🏗️ Stacking at Scale: Real-world frontier models do not rely on just one block — they stack 32 to 96+ of these identical transformer blocks directly on top of each other! For example, Llama 3 8B stacks 32 layers, GPT-3 stacks 96 layers, and Llama 3 405B stacks 126 layers. Early layers identify basic syntax and punctuation, middle layers resolve entity relationships and facts, and deep layers handle multi-step reasoning and abstract synthesis.
